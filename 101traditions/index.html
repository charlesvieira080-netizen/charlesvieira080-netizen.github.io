Making Room for Tradition

How a family or cultural tradition goes on the clock: where in the night, how long it really runs, who explains it to the room, and what the DJ needs to get it right.

How does a cultural tradition fit into the reception, and how does the DJ handle it? It fits wherever the family says it fits, it runs longer than anyone tells you, and the DJ handles it by asking questions early and then getting out of the way. I am not going to explain your tradition to you. You know it, your grandmother knows it better, and the worst thing anyone on a microphone can do is explain a family's custom to that family. What I can tell you is what a tradition needs from the timeline, from the room, and from the person running the music, because New Bedford is a port city, the families here came from everywhere, and every season brings something to the floor that the venue has never seen. Here is how it goes on the clock.

Where in the night

There are four good slots. Right after the grand entrance, while everyone is seated and paying attention: this is where a ceremony-like moment goes, tea served to your parents, a blessing in another language, a presentation to the elders. Between dinner and the cake, in the table-visit gap: a quiet moment, a song the family sings. Right after the cake and the parent dances, as the door into open dancing: this is where the big, loud, everyone-up traditions go, a circle dance the whole room joins, the couple lifted on chairs the way a hora does it, a line with a leader, because the room is already on its feet. And late, inside open dancing, where the money dance and anything that needs a packed floor live. What does not work is dropping a seated, serious moment into the middle of a packed floor. You lose the floor and you lose the moment.

How long it really runs

Ask the person who has done it at the last three family weddings, then add ten minutes. A tradition the family says takes five takes fifteen once the elders are found, seated, and photographed. That is not a problem, it is a number, and it goes on the run of show. Everything you keep here comes out of open dancing, so keep the ones that matter and put them where the energy helps them.

Who explains it to the room

A family member, on a mic, in one or two sentences, and then it starts. Half your guests may never have seen it, and the ones who have want it done right, not narrated. The MC's job is to introduce that person, hand over the mic, and make sure the room can hear. If the family would rather nobody explain it, that is fine too; the guests will figure it out.

What the person running the music needs

  • The actual recording, as a file, two weeks out. Not a title, not a link to a video: the file, and a second copy. If the music is live (a family member, an instrument, a singer), they need a mic, a sound check, and a cue.
  • The name of the tradition and how to say it, plus the name of every person called up and how to say it. Pronunciation is respect; it goes on the sheet.
  • Who leads it. Every circle, line, or chair lift has a person who starts it, and the MC needs to know who, so the music starts when they do.
  • The cue: what happens right before, and what the music does at the end. Fade, hard stop, or straight into the next song?
  • What the room needs: the floor cleared for a circle, sturdy chairs (not folding ones) for a lift, a table and a tray for the tea, a mic on a stand for a song, space for people in traditional dress to move.

Tell the venue

Anything involving chairs lifted off the ground, open flame, food brought in by the family, a floor covering, or a lot of people moving at once is a venue conversation, weeks out. The venue would rather know, and so would the catering captain, because the kitchen is timed around a room that is on its feet for twenty minutes.

Respect the thing

Don't shorten it to save the floor; cut something else. Don't let the MC turn it into a bit. Don't let anyone fade the song early because the room looks restless; the room is not the point for those ten minutes. And if two families are bringing two traditions, give each its own slot instead of running them back to back, so neither one feels like the opening act.

Add your traditions to the events picker with a duration and a place in the night, and they show up on the run of show where you put them.

Quick answers

  • Where does the money dance go? Inside open dancing, about forty-five minutes in, kept quick. The money dance article has the rest.
  • Can our DJ play it right? If they get the file and the leader's name early, yes. If they get handed a phone at 9 o'clock, that is on all of us.

Make room for it on the clock, hand over the file and the names, and let the family lead.
