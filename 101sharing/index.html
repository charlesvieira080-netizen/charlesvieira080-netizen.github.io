Sharing the Night While It Is Still the Night

A content creator and a videographer do two different jobs on two different clocks. Here is what each one does, and how to plan what gets shared the same night.

Content creator, videographer, or both? Here is the difference in one breath. A videographer makes the film: real cameras, real audio, edited over weeks or months, the thing you watch on your tenth anniversary. A content creator shoots short vertical clips on a phone, candid and close, and edits as the day goes, so the first clip can exist while the party is still going. One is the keepsake. The other is the conversation. You can have one, both, or neither, and this article is about how to decide, and how to plan what gets shared the same night so it happens on purpose.

Full disclosure, and then I'll leave it alone the way I do on every page of this course: these days, holding the phone at a wedding is part of my own job. I spent twenty years in the middle of the room running the night, and I have never once done what a videographer does. Nobody is selling anything here.

Which one do you need?

If you want a film with the vows in full and your grandmother's laugh in real audio, hire a videographer. If you want your wedding in your own feed while people are still talking about it, hire a content creator. If you want both, hire both; the good ones work around the photographer and each other. And if you want neither, that is a real choice, and a common one. The unplugged article is for you.

How the day gets shared while it is fresh

Left alone, this happens by accident: a cousin posts a blurry aisle shot before you have seen it, and the guests' photos scatter across forty phones with no home. A sharing plan is four decisions, made before the day and written where your team can see them.

  1. Who posts first. The two of you, or nobody until you do. Say it once on the website and once from the microphone, and people respect it.
  2. What goes out the same night. Usually one sneak peek: a clip or a photo of the two of you that you have both seen and both like. That is the one that gets shared a thousand times, so choose it.
  3. What waits. The ceremony, the vows, the toasts, anything with a guest who did not ask to be posted. Waiting a day costs nothing.
  4. Where the guests' photos go. One link, on a card at every table, that opens a shared gallery with no app to download. Guests with a job (take a picture, drop it here) have more fun, and you wake up to a gallery instead of a scavenger hunt.

Privacy is a guest question, not a tech question

Someone on your list does not want to be online: a teacher, a nurse, a person in the middle of something private. Ask on the RSVP or the website, keep a short list, and hand it to whoever is shooting. The ceremony is the moment most guests want kept off the internet even when they are fine with the reception, which is one more reason the unplugged article exists.

The livestream, for the people who can't be there

A livestream of the ceremony is a normal thing to offer now, and it fails the same three ways every time: no signal, no sound, and no owner. Test the signal at the site before the day. Feed the officiant's microphone into it, because a phone in row six hears wind and nothing else. And give it to one person who has no other job during the ceremony. Not the officiant, not a parent, not the photographer.

What the person holding the phone needs from you

The run of show, the names of the people who matter, the three moments you most want to see again, and a place to stand that is not in the photographer's frame. The photographer's shot comes first. That was my rule from the other side of the room for twenty years, and it has not changed. The same goes for your guests: the aisle belongs to the person you hired to shoot it, and the first dance is better through eyes than through a screen. Say that from the mic once, kindly, and the room will mostly listen.

The clock

A content creator moves through the day like a guest with a job: getting ready, the ceremony, cocktail hour, the reception, editing in the gaps. The gaps are the point. If your timeline has no gaps (see the pace article), the same-night clip becomes a next-morning clip. The last dance and the sendoff close the night's reel, which is one more reason to plan the ending on purpose.

What usually goes wrong here

The clip posted before the couple saw it. The guest gallery link on one sign by the door nobody read. The livestream on a phone propped on a chair, pointed at the sun. All three have a fix above, and none of the fixes cost anything.

Decide who shoots, decide who posts, decide where the photos live. Then put the phone down and get married.
