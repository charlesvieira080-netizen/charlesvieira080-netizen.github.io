The Cocktail Hour

What your guests are doing while you take pictures, why an hour is the right length, and the touches that keep the room warm until the doors open.

They eat, they drink, they find their people, and they wait for you. That's cocktail hour from where I stand, and the waiting is the part you can control. An hour is the right length. It's called cocktail hour for a reason, and by minute 75 a room starts checking the time and the bar line. If your photos need more than an hour, that's a first look conversation, not a longer cocktail hour. This article covers what makes the hour feel like a party instead of a lobby, what I'm doing during it, and what goes wrong.

BACKGROUND MUSIC IS NOT OPTIONAL

This should be a given, and it isn't always. Music makes a room feel occupied before it's full, and it tells people what kind of night this is going to be. I play it at conversation level, and I match it to the two of you: if cocktail hour is soul and the reception is going to be a dance party, the room already knows something is coming. If cocktail hour is on a patio and my rig is in the ballroom, I need a second speaker out there, and I need to know that before the day, not at 4:45. Tell your entertainment director where the guests will actually be standing.

THINGS THAT GET PEOPLE TALKING

Something to sign. A framed print from your engagement session on an easel with a wide matte, and a pen. It doesn't have to be a photo: a surfboard, a map of the place you met, a guitar. Guests get a kick out of signing something odd.

Words of wisdom cards. Note cards, pens, and a garbage can. Name on one side, best advice for a long marriage on the other. Keep them, or have the MC read the good ones during dinner (that's in the meal article).

A canvas. Paint, brushes, and an invitation for every guest to leave a mark. Great idea, many considerations: smocks, a drop cloth, and someone who owns it.

Photo table numbers. Pictures of the two of you holding up each table's number in places that mean something. Guests wander the room looking for the funny ones, and wandering guests are talking guests.

Somewhere to put the pictures. A sign with a link to a shared album, right where the guest book is. Cocktail hour is when the phones come out; give the pictures a home. The phones article has the wording.

Show up. If you did a first look, you can go to your own cocktail hour, and I recommend it. Twenty minutes in the room and nobody is asking where you are, and your grand entrance still works, because the doors close and the room resets.

WHAT I'M DOING

Cocktail hour is the busiest quiet hour of my night. The music's on. I'm watching the photographer for the ten-minute warning, checking the mic, confirming with the caterer when the doors open and what dinner looks like from the kitchen's side, and then herding: finding every member of the wedding party, getting them to the hallway in the order we agreed, checking every name one more time. The herding is the job nobody sees.

When the doors open, the guests find their seats (give that fifteen minutes; twenty-five if cocktail hour is in a different room or the count is over two hundred), and only then does the grand entrance start.

WHAT USUALLY GOES WRONG HERE

  • The photos run to ninety minutes. The guests don't know why; they know the bar line.
  • One bar for 150 people. If the venue offers a second bar or a wine-and-beer table, take it. The line is the thing people remember.
  • Nobody closed the doors, so half the room is already seated when I go to line up the party. Somebody owns the doors.
  • The wedding party is at the bar when I need them in the hallway. I plan for it, and I still lose one every few weddings.

QUESTIONS I GET ASKED

Do we have to have a cocktail hour?

No. If you did a first look and the ceremony is on site, thirty to forty-five minutes of drinks and passed food is enough, and the night gets that time back for dancing.

Can guests dance during cocktail hour?

They won't, and don't ask them to. Cocktail hour is for talking. Save the floor.

What if the ceremony and reception are in the same room?

Then cocktail hour is the room flip, and it needs the full hour, sometimes more. Ask the venue how long the turn takes and believe them.

Prepare each thing you add, have a backup for the ones that depend on weather or paint, and keep the hour an hour. The reception starts the moment the doors open, and a warm room walks through them.
