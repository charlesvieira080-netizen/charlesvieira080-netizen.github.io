The Last Dance

End on purpose: pick the last song before the day, put it five minutes before the music has to stop, keep one in your pocket for an encore, and choose the circle, the private dance, or both.

As the saying goes, all good things must come to an end, and wedding receptions are no different. The question is whether your ending is forgettable or the thing people talk about on the way home.

You end on purpose by deciding four things before the day: the last song, the exact time it plays (five minutes before the venue's hard stop for music), whether there's an encore hiding behind it, and the shape of the room when it happens: everyone in a circle around you, just the two of you in an empty room, or one and then the other.

Fast and yours, or slow and intimate, there is no wrong answer. There is only the ending nobody planned, and I have watched that one too many times.

LEAVE 'EM WANTING MORE

At the end of a great night the guests boo the last song and chant for one more. If that happens, your entertainment did its job. There's a reason most movies come in under two hours, and most concerts too. Knowing when to say when is one of the most important parts of entertaining anyone.

It applies to your reception: if the room peaks at 9:40 and the contract says 10:30, end at 10:00 with the whole room on the floor rather than at 10:30 with twenty people and the lights coming up. From the booth, I'm watching the floor from about ninety minutes out. If it's thinning, I'll suggest moving the last dance up, and I tell you first, never the room.

THE CLOCK

Ask the venue for two times, not one: when the music has to stop and when everyone has to be out. The last song goes at music-off minus five. The sendoff needs about ten minutes after it (see The Big Sendoff), so the guests start lining up for it while the last song is still playing, and the driver already knows the time.

Keep the last dance in the room. That song and the sendoff are the last thirty seconds of the reel whoever is capturing your night is going to cut, so the person holding the camera needs the plan too.

THE ENCORE

Just like every memorable show, the last song is not always the last song. Announce a slow one as the last dance. When it ends, the MC asks the room if they want one more (they do), and the DJ plays the upbeat closer that's been held back all night. It works because the crowd thinks they earned it. It only works once.

THE CIRCLE

This is my default. The MC invites everyone to make one big circle around the two of you for the last dance, and the whole room is in the same moment at the same time. It looks great on camera, and it gives you two options inside it: the MC walks the circle with a wireless mic while guests say their best wishes to you, or the two of you walk the circle saying goodbye to every person who stayed to the end. A big room needs two songs for that. Plan for it.

THE PRIVATE LAST DANCE

The newer option, and I like it a lot. The guests head outside to line up for the sendoff, the room empties, and the DJ plays one song for just the two of you, with the photographer and whoever is capturing content the only people left. Five minutes. It's the one quiet minute the whole day never gave you.

The trade is real, though: the room is empty for your last song. If you want the circle and the private dance both, do the circle first, then send the room out and take your minute. The last song block on the DJ Sheet asks for the song, slow-then-encore or one closer, circle or not, private dance or not, and prints it for whoever runs your music.

WHAT USUALLY GOES WRONG HERE

The lights come on at the hour sharp while the song is still going. The last song runs into the noise cutoff and gets cut. The two of you are outside for a photo during your own last dance. Or nobody picked the song, so the night ends on whatever was playing. Every one of those is decided the week before, not at 9:55.

QUESTIONS I GET ASKED

Should the last song be slow or fast?

Slow if it's about the two of you, fast if it's about the room, and slow-then-fast if you want both. I lean toward ending on energy, but a slow last song with everyone in a circle is the one I've seen produce the most tears.

Can we end earlier than the contract?

Yes, and you should if the room has peaked. You paid for the option, not the obligation. Just make sure the venue, the caterer, and the driver hear the new time from one person.

What if people are still going strong at the hard stop?

Then you did it right, and the answer is the after-party, not an argument with the venue. The MC announces where, the sendoff happens, and the night continues somewhere else.

The last dance is a big chance to put your personal touch on the reception and share one moment with everyone who stayed. Prepare it, plan it, and have a backup ready.
