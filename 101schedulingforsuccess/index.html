Wedding 101 · Building the agenda

Schedule For Success

The order of a reception and how long every piece takes, with the math I use to build a night that keeps guests informed, involved, and entertained.

What is the order of events at a reception, and how long does each one take? Here is the version I have used for twenty years: cocktail hour (60 minutes), guests seated (15), grand entrance (10), first dance (5), welcome or blessing (5), toasts (10), dinner (45 to 75, depending on how it is served and how many people you have), about 30 minutes of table visits, cake cutting (5), parent dances (10), and then open dancing until the last song. Add it up from your ceremony and you know when the floor opens. A badly paced reception is almost always a badly planned agenda, and if the agenda is not planned around the entertainment, what looks like fun on paper may not be so fun in real life. So, the science: an agenda that keeps your guests informed, involved, and entertained.

Best ideas, not all the good ideas

One very common mistake is packing the agenda with too many events. It is usually done to prevent boredom, and it produces a top-heavy night. Too much of a good thing is still too much, and more is not always better (especially in this business). Hold on to the best ideas rather than all of the good ideas.

Your venue is a great resource; they know what works in that room. Make sure they are not going to start cleaning up before your reception ends. I've seen a manager turn the lights on before the last song, and that is an invitation for guests to leave thinking the party is over. Go over the timeline with your venue.

The order, and the math

Every timeline I build runs forward from the ceremony, in five-minute steps. Ceremony, about 30 minutes. Cocktail hour starts when guests reach the reception site and runs an hour; if your photos need more than that (no first look, a lot of family groupings), the hour stretches to cover them and your guests wait, which is the first thing that goes wrong on most timelines. Seating the room takes 15 minutes, or 25 if cocktail hour is in a different room or you have 200 or more people. Then the grand entrance, the first dance, the welcome or blessing, and the toasts, which I put before dinner while everyone is seated and paying attention. Two speakers at four minutes each is ten; past fifteen, the room is looking at the kitchen.

Dinner is the piece couples guess wrong, because it depends on how it is served and how many people you have. My defaults: plated two courses, 60 minutes; three courses, 75; buffet, 45; family style, 50; stations, 60; a cocktail-style reception, 45. Add ten minutes for every 50 guests over a hundred, and ten more for a buffet with a single line. A plated dinner for 200 people is 80 minutes, not 60.

Then the cake. I always recommend letting about a half hour pass between the last plate and the cutting; let the two of you make your rounds. Served right at the end of the meal, the cake tends to go uneaten (and they are not cheap) because everyone is still full. Wait a bit and they develop a sweet tooth. The cutting itself is five minutes. Then the parent dances, right after the cake, while everyone is still seated and watching you. Then the floor opens, and everything left before the last song belongs to it.

That last number is the one to protect. On a five-hour night, this order usually leaves the floor ninety minutes or more. When the math gives you less, something upstream has to give: toasts before dinner instead of between courses, one parent song instead of two, a first look that shortens the photos, or a later end time. What you don't do is squeeze the dinner. The caterer can't serve faster than the kitchen.

The moments inside the dancing

The bouquet toss, an anniversary dance, a money dance, a slideshow: these are options, not requirements, and each one is a reason for the floor to stop. Place them where they add energy. A toss at the start of open dancing works; a slow money dance dropped into a packed floor sends everyone to their seats. And calling only single people to the floor has fallen out of favor. Nobody wants their status announced at a wedding.

Around here the grand entrance usually runs straight into the first dance. The order also changes with region, culture, and family; if yours has a tradition in it, the traditions article puts it on the clock. The morning (hair, makeup, who arrives when) is its own clock; the week-of article covers it.

The ending

Decide when to call it. Unless your venue has a hard stop (many do), the ending is up to you. Don't get trapped into thinking the reception has to last as long as you have the room; end early if it makes a better send-off. The last song goes at the hard stop minus five, and the send-off after it.
