Other Special Touches

The extras guests actually remember are the ones that let them connect with you and each other: a dozen that work, what each costs in minutes, and the rule for how many to keep.

Your guests come to your wedding because they care about you and want to celebrate with you. What they remember afterward is not the centerpieces. It's the moments where they got to connect with the two of you and with each other, and it's a short list: one or two things, not seven.

Guests leave a reception early for one reason, they aren't enjoying themselves, and the fastest way to bore a room is to make them spectators. The fastest way to bore them in the other direction is to stack so many bits on the night that the dance floor never gets going. Here are the extras that have worked in my rooms, what each one costs in minutes, and the rule for choosing.

THE RULE FIRST

Every one of these is a reason for the room to sit down. When the moments inside open dancing add up past about thirty minutes, the floor you worked all night to fill starts to empty, and it doesn't come back the same. Hold on to the best ideas rather than all of the good ideas.

FROM THE OLD PLAYBOOK, STILL WORKING

  • The family unity candle. Three candles on the head table. Someone from each family lights the outside two before the grand entrance (a parent, a grandparent, someone who hasn't had a role yet). When the two of you walk in and the applause starts to fade, you light the center one together and the room cheers again. Two minutes, and the MC explains it in one line.
  • The coloring contest. Little ones at a wedding need a job. A kids' table with coloring pages and crayons, the MC gets them started, a small prize for every entry and something extra for the winner. It costs nothing on the clock and buys their parents an hour.
  • Karaoke. If your crowd sings more than it dances, do it in sets between dance sets, or make it the main event. It needs gear beyond what a DJ normally carries and, more important, a host who can keep a room alive between singers, so ask who that is before you book it.

THE ONES I'VE ADDED SINCE

  • The audio guestbook. An old phone on a stand; guests pick it up and leave you a message. Put it where people walk past, not in a corner, and have the MC send tables to it during dinner. You'll play those back for years.
  • Guest photos in one place. A shared album with a code on the tables, mentioned once from the mic. It replaces the disposable cameras we used to hand out, and you have the photos that night. The ceremony is a different question; see The Unplugged Ceremony.
  • Late-night food. Pizza, fries, sliders, a truck outside, timed for the hour the bar slows down. It keeps the room from thinning out at 9:30, and it's the thing people mention on Monday.
  • The shoe game. Two chairs back to back, one of each other's shoes in your hands, and the MC asks questions: who said I love you first, who's the better driver. You hold up the shoe of the answer. Ten minutes, and the biggest laugh of the night if the questions are yours and not from a list on the internet.
  • A slideshow. Three to five minutes, no longer, during dinner or a parent dance, on a file tested on two devices and on the venue's screen before the day. The untested file is the one that fails. Every time.
  • The centerpiece giveaway. The MC picks a rule at each table (the birthday closest to today, the longest drive) and that person takes the centerpiece home. Five minutes, and a nice way to end dinner.
  • A surprise. A friend's band for two songs, a number by the wedding party, a song one of you wrote. Tell the MC and the person capturing content, and nobody else.

WHAT USUALLY GOES WRONG HERE

The slideshow file that won't open on the venue's laptop. The audio guestbook nobody found. Karaoke that swallowed the dance floor whole. Five bits stacked between the cake and the last hour, so the room spent the night sitting down. All of it is fixable the week before, none of it on the night.

QUESTIONS I GET ASKED

How many extras is too many?

If the moments inside open dancing pass thirty minutes total, you've crossed it. Two well-planned extras beat five average ones.

Where do these go in the night?

The candle at the entrance, the kids' table during cocktail hour and dinner, the slideshow during dinner, the shoe game and the giveaway at the end of dinner, and late-night food an hour before the last song.

Do we tell the MC about all of it?

Every single one, with a time and a name attached. The one the MC didn't know about is the one that happens in silence.

These are the extras you sprinkle in for flavor. Choose the few that sound like the two of you, put them on the agenda, and let the rest of the night be the dance floor.
