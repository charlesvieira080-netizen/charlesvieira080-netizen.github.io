The toast rule that saves the reception.

Two to four speakers. Three to four minutes each. Fifteen minutes, all in. It is the simplest rule in wedding planning, and it protects the best twenty minutes of your night.

The rule

Fifteen minutes, all in.

After twenty years of receptions, the pattern is not subtle. The toasts that people still talk about at the after-party were short, sincere and rehearsed. The ones that emptied the bar line into the hallway were long, improvised and stacked five speakers deep.

  • Two to four speakers. Usually the best man, the maid or matron of honor, and a parent or two. Everyone else who loves you gets the dance floor, the photo booth and the whole rest of the night to say it in person.
  • Three to four minutes each. That is a full page of writing read aloud. It is longer than it sounds and exactly as long as a room stays leaned in.
  • Fifteen minutes, all in. The kitchen is holding dinner on this window. Keep it, and the food lands hot and the room stays warm.
Why it works

A room can hold its breath only so long.

A toast is the one moment of the reception where everybody does the same thing at once: stop, look at one table, and listen. That attention is the most valuable thing in the room, and it is not unlimited. The rule spends it where it counts and hands the night back before anyone starts checking the bar line.

Short also protects your speakers. A nervous best man with a three minute page in his hand finishes strong. The same best man told to "just say a few words" finds out live that a few words is the hardest length there is.

How to hand the rule to your people

Say it early, blame us if you need to.

Give the assignment with the ask

The moment you ask someone to speak, give them the container: "about three minutes, we have a fifteen minute window before dinner." Nobody is offended by a clear assignment. People are offended by being cut off at minute nine, which is what happens without one.

Put it in writing once

A week out, one short message to all your speakers with the order and the timing. Written down, the rule is logistics. Said out loud for the first time on the day, it is criticism.

Let the MC be the bad guy

This is half of what an MC is for. We set the order with your speakers before the room sits down, we cue each one so nobody is left standing with a warm microphone wondering when to start, and if you want a hard limit enforced, we are delighted to be the reason. Your uncle stays your favorite uncle.
