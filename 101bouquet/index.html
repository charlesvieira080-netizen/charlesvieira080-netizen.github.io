Bouquet/Garter Toss

Do a toss only if you want one: how the bouquet and garter toss actually run, the minutes each costs, and the alternatives, starting with the anniversary dance, that I reach for first.

Should you do a toss? Only if you want one, and most of the couples I work with now either skip it or change it. The bouquet toss still shows up plenty; the garter has become the exception. Neither is required, and nothing else on your agenda depends on them. A bouquet toss costs about ten minutes with the gathering and the photos, a garter five, and the alternative I reach for first, the anniversary dance, five.

What has changed isn't the flowers. It's that people no longer want their single status announced to a room, so the version that works in 2026 is the one where anyone who wants to catch it can.

The story goes that guests once tore pieces off the wedding clothes for luck, and tossing a bouquet (and later a garter) was the couple's way of giving the crowd something to grab and being left alone. Whether or not that's how it started, that's still what it is: a little burst of energy, a photo, and a laugh. Treat it like that and it works. Treat it like a ceremony and it dies on the floor.

THE STRAIGHT TOSS, IF YOU WANT IT

From the booth, it takes some choreography. I place it at the start of open dancing or right after a low-energy moment (the cake, the money dance), so it lifts the room instead of interrupting it. The MC gathers whoever wants to catch it, no categories, with a song that has a count-in, and the photographer is set before anyone throws anything.

You use a toss bouquet, not the real one, and you throw it over your shoulder, not at the chandelier (I have seen both). If the floor is already packed and the night is rolling, do it from the middle of the floor and don't stop the party for it.

The garter, if you keep it, is a chair, one song, and a smile, and then it's over. The stunts that used to go with it (I have done every one of them, years ago) are gone from my playbook, and nobody has asked for them back.

THE ALTERNATIVES I REACH FOR FIRST

  • The anniversary dance. Every married couple in the room comes to the floor for a slow song. I count up the years on the mic, and couples sit down as their number passes. The last couple standing gets the bouquet, a round of applause, and one question on the mic: what's the secret? That answer is the best thirty seconds of a lot of my nights.
  • The presentation. Hand the bouquet to someone on purpose: the friend who just got engaged, a grandparent, the person who held you up this year. Tell the MC who, so the music and the room are ready.
  • The kids' toss. If there are little ones in the room, a stuffed animal toss with a candy song under it, and a photo with whoever catches it, buys you five minutes of the loudest cheering of the night.
  • The open toss. Keep the bouquet, drop the rules. Anyone who wants it comes up. Same ten minutes, none of the awkward part.

Whichever you pick, tell your MC exactly who is involved and when, and pick a song. The keep, skip, or alternative card in Choosing Your Events holds the decision and the minutes it costs, and it feeds the Run of Show so the moment has a place in the night.

WHAT USUALLY GOES WRONG HERE

Three people on the floor because half the guests are at the bar, which is why the MC gathers the room before the music starts. A garter bit that runs longer than your first dance. A photographer in the restroom because nobody gave them a heads-up. And a toss placed at the end of the night, after the people you wanted on the floor have gone home.

QUESTIONS I GET ASKED

When in the night should a toss happen?

Early in open dancing, or right after the cake or the money dance. Late enough that the floor is warm, early enough that the people you want on it are still there.

Can we do one toss and not the other?

Of course. Most couples who keep anything keep the bouquet alone, or swap both for the anniversary dance.

What if we want the garter but not the show?

Then it's a chair, a song, thirty seconds, and a toss. Tell me that's the version, and that's the version you get.

There are more ways to do this than I can list, and none of them require calling out the single people. With a little planning, a moment that used to embarrass half the room becomes a fun one.
