The Big Sendoff

Plan the last scene: the sendoff type, the ten minutes it takes, who lines the guests up, what your venue allows, and why sparklers are off the table in Massachusetts.

After your last dance, you can say goodbye to your guests one at a time, or you can wave to all of them at once as they give you a sendoff to remember. Planning it takes five decisions: the type, the time (about ten minutes, right after the last song), who lines the guests up (the MC and your coordinator), what you're walking out to (a car, a shuttle, the after-party), and what your venue allows, which you ask in writing before you buy a single thing.

On that last point, one Massachusetts fact up front: sparklers are fireworks under state law here, so plan a sendoff that doesn't need a flame. This is the last article in the guide and the last scene of the movie, so let's shoot it right.

WHY BOTHER WITH A SENDOFF AT ALL

The last dance gives your guests closure. The sendoff gives it to you. Do you want the final memory of your reception to be loading presents into a trunk and watching the centerpieces get boxed, or walking out through a hundred people cheering? It's also the shot that closes the reel: whoever is capturing your day wants the two of you walking toward the camera through whatever the guests are holding.

WHAT THE VENUE ALLOWS

Every venue has a list, and it comes down to three questions: what can go on the ground, what can be thrown, and what can be lit. Real petals are often outdoors-only or not allowed at all; some venues want silk, some want nothing that has to be swept. Rice gets banned for the slipping hazard on a wet night. Bubbles are almost always fine. Confetti is almost never fine.

And sparklers: the Massachusetts fireworks statute lists sparklers by name among the fireworks that nobody may sell, possess, or use here without the state license and permits. Venues know it. Some allow a rented cold-spark machine run by a licensed company instead, and that is the venue's call and the company's paperwork, not yours. Ask, get the answer in writing, and then buy supplies.

THE SHAPES THAT WORK

  • The human tunnel. No supplies, no permission needed. The MC lines your guests up in two rows from the dance floor to the door, arms up, and the two of you run (or walk, your call) through the tunnel while they cheer. It works in any room, and it's my pick when the weather turns.
  • Bubbles. Little bottles handed out at the door, a cloud outside, and photos that look better than they have any right to.
  • Light. Glow sticks, light-up wands, or ribbon wands. They photograph like sparklers, they're legal, and nobody burns a sleeve.
  • Petals. Paper cones of petals, when the venue says yes. Outdoors, under good light, they're the classic.
  • The paparazzi exit. Every guest with a phone, flashlights on, both sides of the walk. The disposable cameras we used to hand out are gone; the shared album with a code on the tables does the same job, and you have the photos that night.

HOW IT RUNS FROM THE BOOTH

I tell the room at the cake, and again before the last dance, that there's a sendoff and where to be, because the thing that most often ruins one is half the guests leaving before it happens. Ten minutes before the last song ends, the MC sends everyone out to line up and the coordinator hands out the supplies at the door (one per guest plus ten percent; the picker counts it from your guest count).

The photographer and whoever is capturing content go out first and set up. The MC keeps the two of you inside until the line is ready, which takes two or three minutes, and the car is already at the curb because the driver had the time on paper. Then you go. Start to finish, about ten minutes.

The sendoff card in Choosing Your Events holds the type, the venue's yes, the supplies count, and the ten-minute rule, and it prints on the vendor copy of your run of show.

WHAT USUALLY GOES WRONG HERE

The car isn't there. Nobody told the guests. The supplies are in someone's trunk. Or the sendoff happens and then the reception keeps going, which confuses everyone. If you want the photo version early (some couples do it at last light and come back in to dance), fine, as long as the MC tells the room it isn't goodbye.

QUESTIONS I GET ASKED

What time should the sendoff be?

Right after the last song, before the music-off time, and before the venue's everyone-out time by enough that nobody is stacking chairs behind you.

What if it rains?

The human tunnel inside, or bubbles under the entrance overhang. Put it in your rain plan so nobody decides it in the moment.

Do we need a coordinator for this?

You need one person who isn't you holding the supplies and watching the door. A coordinator, a parent, a friend with a loud voice. Name them on the Team Sheet.

Use every opportunity you have, the sendoff included, to make your mark on your own wedding. If you've read all of these articles, you now know why a wedding is a production, why there's only one take, and how to make yours the one your guests talk about for years. Good luck.
